USTP Jasaan Campus Setting CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION Background of the Study The introduction of new technology has changed the way colleges and universities operate, especially in the handling of academic work and research. These projects are a compulsory requirement to demonstrate the studentâ€™s ability to apply creative thinking to solve real-world problems. But for many institutions, systems are not yet up to date or organized, and student work is often kept in paper files or scattered across different apps (Perante et al., 2024) . Without a central repository it is difficult to track progress, to find similar research, or to see that topics are not being re-covered (Reyes et al., 2024). Studies have shown that many of these problems are caused by the absence of a unified system. Manual monitoring has been associated with administrative delays and lack of proper communication between students and advisors (Lando &amp; Wambua, 202 5 ). Similarly, Huang (202 5 ) found that colleges find it difficult to communicate their findings to external partners because they lack formal technology transfer procedures. Some systems help to structure data to perform office tasks, for example, the one built by Liu et al. (2021), but usually do not allow the real-world use of research. Ossa et al. (2025) further found that progress tracking often fails because schools donâ€™t have real data or clear plans to get students to finish on time. The research imperative for this study was established after observing that at the University of Science and Technology of Southern Philippines (USTP) â€“ Jasaan Campus , many student inventions are underutilized because there is no formal way to connect them with industry or community partners. Currently, advisors and staff often update records by hand through unorganized channels, which leads to confusion and delays in project development. This study aims to solve these problems by proposing a single, tech-based tool to make research management easier and more transparent. The proposed system offers several advantages over existing manual methods by providing a centralized digital home for all research project data. It includes real-time progress tracking through goals and timelines, allowing advisors to provide structured feedback and generate automatic reports. Most importantly, the system features a "technology transfer" section designed to match student projects with the right partners and track how these inventions are used in the real world. To ensure its effectiveness, the system will be evaluated using ISO/IEC 25010 quality standards to check its functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, reliability, and security . Furthermore, the evaluation will employ User Acceptance Testing (UAT) and the System Usability Scale (SUS) to clearly measure user satisfaction, workflow integration, and overall ease of use in order to ensure that the platform is useful and intuitive for its intended users. The process illustrates how project data and user information are transformed into organized, reliable outputs that enhance institutional oversight and facilitate the real-world application of research. Statement of the Problem General Problem Higher education institutions struggle with decentralized research management and inconsistent progress monitoring due to underutilized digital tools (Hailitik et al., 2025). Furthermore, a lack of structured technology transfer and weak industry ties prevent academic discoveries from reaching real-world application, particularly in Philippine state universities (Goncharenko, 2021; Sarita, 2025). Therefore, this study addresses the urgent need for a centralized system to bridge the gap between academic output and industry utilization. Specific Problems Absence of a digital inventory repository for organizing and managing thesis and capstone project outputs. Lack of an efficient system for monitoring the progress of ongoing research and development projects. Limited mechanisms for facilitating technology transfer between academic institutions and industry or community partners. Difficulty in generating comprehensive dashboards and reports for research tracking, extension activities, and collaboration. Need for a system that meets quality standards in terms of functionality, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and security based on ISO/IEC 25010. It incorporates a progress tracking feature that allows students, advisors, and administrative staff to monitor project development through established milestones and timelines, ensuring timely completion of academic requirements. The system is designed to support multiple user roles including students, advisors, staff, and external partners e ach with specific access levels and functional tasks. Limitations of the Study Despite its intended contributions, this study is subject to several limitations. The system is developed specifically for the University of Science and Technology of Southern Philippines â€“ Jasaan Campus and is not currently intended for use by other institutions. Consequently, its features are tailored to the specific administrative rules and workflows of the Jasaan campus. The system does not incorporate Artificial Intelligence (AI) functionalities, such as automated recommendations, predictive analytics, plagiarism detection, or AI-based monitoring tools. Instead, all system operations rely on manual user input, predefined task sequences, and human evaluation. This focus ensures the study remains centered on the core functional architecture of the management system rather than advanced automation technologies. Furthermore, while the platform includes a technology transfer module, it cannot guarantee that external partners will officially launch or adopt the projects, as such outcomes depend on external factors beyond the system's control. The scope of management is strictly limited to student thesis and capstone projects and does not extend to faculty research, academic journals, or projects funded by external organizations. In terms of technical features, the system does not facilitate student consultations or offer advanced real-time communication tools like live chat or video conferencing, as the primary focus is strictly on organization, milestone tracking, and reporting . The reliability of the generated results depends entirely on the accuracy and completeness of the data entered by users. Additionally, integration with third-party platforms such as Google Scholar or Scopus may be limited due to technical and resource constraints. Lastly, while basic security measures are implemented, the system may not be protected against all advanced cybersecurity risks. The evaluation is limited to specific ISO/IEC 25010 standards and short-term user testing, rather than a long-term assessment of how the system performs over an extended period or on a very large scale. 1. 7 Definition of Terms To ensure clarity and a shared understanding of the concepts discussed in this study, the following terms are operationally defined based on their specific use within the proposed system: Adviser and Panel Member: Faculty users assigned to supervise project workflows, provide structured feedback on milestone submissions, and utilize the system to calculate and record final defense grades. CHAPTER II REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE This chapter presents a review of related literature and studies relevant to the study . A comprehensive examination of both foreign and local sources was conducted to understand existing systems, practices, and approaches in research repositories, project management, academic monitoring, and technology transfer. This review provides a foundation for the study by identifying recent developments, key features, and limitations, particularly in terms of integration, efficiency, and real-world application. It also highlights existing gaps such as the lack of centralized platforms, manual monitoring, and limited technology transfer, which support the need for a more integrated and effective solution. 2.1 Research Repository and Inventory Systems Research repository and inventory systems are essential in higher education because they preserve academic outputs and make institutional knowledge easier to retrieve, verify, and reuse. For thesis and capstone projects, repository systems serve not only as storage locations but also as evidence of student learning, departmental productivity, and institutional research direction. The common problem addressed by the studies in this area is the continued dependence of many institutions on hardbound manuscripts, scattered digital files, and manual recordkeeping practices. These approaches make retrieval slow, expose documents to physical damage or loss, and limit the ability of students, faculty, and administrators to trace previous works. The reviewed studies therefore share a common objective: to improve the organization, accessibility, and protection of academic records through digital systems. Perante et al. (2024) directly addressed this issue by designing and developing a thesis and capstone project manuscript archiving management system for Eastern Visayas State University Ormoc Campus. The study was motivated by the limitations of traditional hardbound storage, particularly the difficulty of preserving, searching, and retrieving completed manuscripts. Using system development approaches associated with Agile and Rapid Application Development, the researchers created a web-based digital archive intended to support faster access to student research outputs. The results showed that digital archiving can improve the preservation and accessibility of thesis and capstone manuscripts. This study is significant because it demonstrates how a local higher education institution can shift from physical archives to a more organized digital environment. However, its main limitation is that it treats the manuscript largely as a completed record. It does not deeply address what happens before archiving, such as proposal submission, adviser feedback, progress tracking, revision history, defense evaluation, or post-completion technology transfer. The focus on improving records is also reflected in the work of Reyes et al. (2024), who assessed the efficiency of computerized student record management systems in higher education institutions. While their study was not limited to thesis or capstone manuscripts, it supports the argument that academic institutions need reliable computerized systems to reduce duplication, improve data accuracy, and strengthen long-term record integrity. Their study proposed a computerized management model that could improve administrative efficiency and user interaction. Compared with Perante et al. (2024), Reyes et al. (2024) approached the issue at a broader institutional records level rather than at a thesis-specific level. This broader scope is useful because it highlights the importance of structured data management, but it also reveals a limitation: general student record systems do not fully capture the milestone-driven nature of thesis and capstone projects, where documents pass through proposal, revision, defense, approval, archiving, and possible utilization stages. Liu et al. (2021) expanded the discussion from basic archiving to scientific research project management by designing and applying a science and technology project management information system for educational institutes. Their study analyzed user needs, business requirements, system feasibility, and system-level design, then developed a layered architecture to support secure and consistent research management. Its contribution lies in showing that research-related information systems must be designed with attention to data consistency, information security, and institutional workflow. In relation to the previous studies, Liu et al. (2021) offered a more technically structured approach than ordinary repository systems. However, the system was designed mainly for scientific research project administration and institutional research office tasks. It does not specifically address the student-centered supervision process of thesis and capstone projects, where advisers, panels, students, department chairs, library personnel, and external partners may need different access levels and workflow responsibilities. The institutional repository perspective is further supported by Safdar (2021), who examined the experience of Library and Information Science professionals in Pakistan in creating institutional repositories using DSpace. The study explored success factors, implementation problems, and possible solutions through multiple phases, including literature review and professional experience-based investigation. Safdar (2021) emphasized that institutional repositories are valuable because they support academic communication, research preservation, and access to scholarly outputs. This strengthens the repository argument made by Perante et al. (2024), but it also introduces an important operational issue: successful implementation depends on staff knowledge, technical capacity, training, and institutional support. Thus, even when a repository platform is available, it may not function effectively if users are not prepared to maintain it. For the current study, this finding is important because a repository should not only be technically functional; it should also be aligned with the roles and actual abilities of the users who will operate it. Doromal and Soberano (2023) presented a more specialized repository system through their Capstone Archiving Management System with Citation and Reference Generator. Their objective was to help students electronically access, search, and use capstone studies while also supporting citation and reference generation. The study involved user acceptance testing among students, faculty, library personnel, and other users familiar with capstone activities. The results showed strong agreement in usefulness, ease of use, and computer system usability, indicating that users found the system helpful and practical. Compared with Safdar (2021), who focused on institutional repository implementation using DSpace, Doromal and Soberano (2023) designed a system closer to student capstone needs. However, despite its usefulness, the system still centers on archiving and reference support. It does not provide a complete active project management process, such as milestone monitoring, adviser review, defense tracking, or technology transfer follow-up. Batoon et al. (2022) moved repository development a step further by integrating plagiarism checking and prescriptive analytics into THESISQUO, a research management system for thesis studies. The study aimed to provide an online repository while also addressing the problem of plagiarism and limited research guidance. Its evaluation using ISO/IEC 25010 showed that the system was positively rated in relevant quality characteristics. This study differs from the earlier archive-centered systems because it attempts to add analytical functions to the repository environment. However, its limitation remains similar: the system is stronger in storing, checking, and analyzing documents than in managing the full thesis or capstone lifecycle. It does not fully integrate day-to-day adviser supervision, milestone-based progress monitoring, department-level reporting, library archiving approval, and structured industry or community partner engagement. 2.2 Monitoring and Evaluation in Academic Systems Monitoring and evaluation systems are important because thesis and capstone projects depend on continuous supervision, timely feedback, milestone completion, and decision-making by advisers, panels, and administrators. While repository systems solve the problem of storing completed outputs, they do not automatically solve delays, weak communication, inconsistent adviser feedback, or lack of follow-up during project development. The studies in this section therefore address a different but related problem: how institutions can track academic progress, identify students or projects at risk, and improve the quality of supervision and evaluation. This section builds on the previous discussion by showing that an effective thesis and capstone management system must support both archiving and active monitoring. Lando and Wambua (2025) examined digital solutions for graduate supervision and provided a critical analysis of thesis management systems. Their work was motivated by supervision challenges such as delayed feedback, weak communication, stagnation of graduate students, and administrative bottlenecks. The objective of the study was to analyze how thesis management systems can improve graduate supervision by organizing supervisor-student interactions and providing clearer oversight. Their discussion showed that digital thesis management systems can strengthen transparency and reduce delays when supervision tasks are supported by structured workflows. This study is closely related to the current research because it confirms that supervision is not only a communication issue but also a management issue. However, its limitation is that it mainly focuses on the graduate supervision relationship and does not fully incorporate repository archiving, library validation, departmental dashboards, or technology transfer mechanisms. Qazdar et al. (2023) approached monitoring from the perspective of learning analytics. Their study proposed student performance indicators based on data generated within Learning Management Systems to support continuous monitoring. The researchers used a case study involving a Web Technologies course on an e-campus platform, transforming student interactions and academic activities into indicators that could help faculty identify underperforming students, project progress, and anticipate academic risk. This study supports the findings of Lando and Wambua (2025) by showing that monitoring becomes more useful when student activity is translated into meaningful indicators. However, Qazdar et al. (2023) focused on course-based learning analytics rather than thesis or capstone project development. A regular course usually follows a predictable class structure, while thesis and capstone projects involve proposal approval, chapter submissions, prototype development, defense scheduling, revisions, final manuscript compliance, and archiving. Therefore, while learning analytics offers useful monitoring principles, these principles must be adapted to the unique workflow of research project management. Regla and Ballera (2023) extended the monitoring discussion to the institutional research productivity level. Their study aimed to design a research data collection system, apply Natural Language Processing techniques to support retrieval and monitoring of research productivity, and generate visualizations and analytics from institutional research data. The study shows how digital systems can help higher education institutions organize research outputs and convert stored information into useful analytics for decision-making. Compared with Qazdar et al. (2023), who focused on individual student progress within an LMS, Regla and Ballera (2023) focused more on institutional research productivity and reporting. This is valuable for administrators because it supports strategic planning and visibility of research performance. Nevertheless, its limitation for the present study is that it monitors productivity at a broader institutional level rather than managing the detailed, project-level activities of students and advisers. It does not fully address how individual thesis groups move from one milestone to another or how adviser feedback and evaluation records are handled during project development. The need for meaningful follow-up is emphasized by Hailitik et al. (2025), who analyzed the effectiveness of academic monitoring and evaluation on the length of study at Nusa Cendana University. Using a qualitative case study approach, the researchers gathered data through in-depth interviews, participant observation, and documentation studies. Their results showed that academic monitoring and evaluation were not fully effective in influencing timely student completion because of minimal academic guidance, weak follow-up on evaluation results, suboptimal use of academic information systems, and limited intervention for delayed students. This finding strengthens the argument that monitoring cannot be reduced to the existence of a system alone. Even when academic information systems are present, they must be actively used to support follow-up, intervention, and accountable decision-making. For the current study, this means that progress monitoring should not simply display status updates; it should create a clearer workflow for submissions, adviser review, milestone tracking, notifications, and reports. Simard (2021) contributed another important perspective by examining the implementation of project management principles in a capstone design course. The study focused on practices such as scheduling, reporting, communication, and structured project organization. Its findings showed that applying project management practices can improve capstone outcomes because students become more guided in planning and completing their work. This study relates closely to Hailitik et al. (2025) because both point to the importance of structure and follow-through. However, Simard (2021) concentrated more on pedagogical project management practices than on a dedicated software platform that can automate, document, and institutionalize these practices. This creates a practical gap: project management principles may improve capstone performance, but without a system that records milestones, stores submissions, tracks evaluations, and generates reports, implementation may still depend heavily on individual advisers and manual coordination. . 2.3 Innovation, Technology Transfer, and Research Advancement Systems Innovation and technology transfer are important extensions of thesis and capstone management because student research is not only an academic requirement but may also contain practical solutions for institutional, community, or industry problems. A project that is properly monitored and archived can still remain underutilized if there is no system for identifying its potential users, recording partner interest, tracking implementation, or reporting its impact. The studies in this section therefore address the problem of connecting academic outputs with broader research advancement and real-world application. This section continues the flow of the review by moving from storage and monitoring toward the final stage of the research lifecycle: utilization, collaboration, and innovation development. Chio et al. (2022) developed ThesisIT, a web-based university thesis management portal with a defense scheduling system. The study was designed to make the thesis cycle easier to follow and complete, particularly by automating oral defense scheduling. The researchers implemented automated scheduling using a genetic algorithm and evaluated the system through usability testing. The results indicated that the system improved administrative efficiency and supported document management and defense scheduling. This study is relevant because it demonstrates how a specific bottleneck in thesis administration, such as scheduling, can be addressed through a digital system. In relation to the monitoring studies discussed earlier, ThesisIT shows the value of automation in reducing delays during the defense stage. However, its limitation is that it focuses mainly on the thesis cycle up to defense-related processes. It does not fully extend to post-defense archiving, technology transfer monitoring, or partner engagement after the project has been completed. Goncharenko (2021) shifted the discussion from thesis administration to the broader university innovation ecosystem. The study proposed a conceptual model of a hackathon ecosystem for technology transfer in a higher education institution. Its objective was to promote collaboration, public visibility, creativity, and the movement of university-generated ideas toward practical application. Unlike Chio et al. (2022), which presented a functional thesis portal, Goncharenko (2021) offered a conceptual ecosystem model centered on innovation events and collaboration. This study is useful because it shows that technology transfer is not merely a database function; it also requires interaction among students, mentors, institutions, and external stakeholders. However, the study remains largely conceptual and does not provide a working software platform for tracking research outputs, monitoring partner interest, or documenting implementation outcomes over time. Huang (2025) further examined technology transfer in higher education by focusing on how data-driven collaboration and institutional processes can strengthen research innovation. The study used survey and workshop-based insights to propose a flexible implementation plan for technology transfer in higher education. This connects with Goncharenko (2021) because both studies recognize that innovation requires structured collaboration between the university and external actors. Huang (2025), however, places stronger attention on data-informed teamwork and implementation planning. Its limitation is that it remains focused on strategy and process improvement rather than on a specific technical platform that links research inventory, progress monitoring, partner matching, and utilization tracking. Thus, while it provides important guidance on how higher education institutions can think about technology transfer, it does not fully solve the system-level problem of managing student research outputs from development to adoption. Palavesm and Joorel (2022) discussed Research Information Management Systems through the implementation of IRINS in Indian higher education institutions. Their study described challenges in setting up research information management, including identifying nodal officers, collecting faculty profile information, retrieving scholarly communication activities from internal and external sources, and providing training to institutional personnel. This study is significant because it shows that research advancement requires organized data infrastructure and trained personnel. Compared with Huang (2025), which emphasized technology transfer planning, Palavesm and Joorel (2022) focused on institutional research information management and visibility. However, IRINS and similar research information systems are generally designed for faculty research profiles, scholarly outputs, and institutional productivity. They are not primarily designed for managing student thesis and capstone projects at the granular level of submissions, adviser reviews, defense outcomes, final repository approval, and partner matching. Sarita (2025) provided a Philippine-specific view of technology transfer in state universities and colleges. The study examined the technology transfer landscape in Philippine SUCs, particularly in the Davao Region and Davao Oriental State University, and highlighted barriers such as weak industry linkages, bureaucratic inefficiencies, limited institutional capacity, regulatory constraints, and inadequate funding. This study is particularly important for the current research because it situates technology transfer within the Philippine higher education context. It supports the idea that student and institutional research outputs may fail to reach practical users not because they lack value, but because universities often lack dedicated mechanisms, personnel, resources, and industry connections to move research toward application. However, while Sarita (2025) provides a strong policy and ecosystem-based analysis, it does not present a software system that can operationalize technology transfer at the level of project records, partner browsing, implementation requests, feedback, and impact tracking. Across these innovation and technology transfer studies, previous researchers have already accomplished important work in three areas. First, they have shown that specific administrative problems in thesis management, such as defense scheduling and document handling, can be improved through web-based systems. Second, they have established that innovation requires structured collaboration between universities and external stakeholders, not only internal academic evaluation. Third, they have demonstrated that research information management systems can increase visibility, organization, and institutional reporting. These accomplishments provide a strong foundation for the present study because they show that digital tools and institutional processes can make academic research more organized and more useful. Despite these accomplishments, common gaps remain across the reviewed studies. Many systems are fragmented, focusing only on one stage of the research lifecycle. Repository systems preserve completed outputs but often neglect active supervision and progress tracking. Monitoring systems improve oversight but usually do not connect their data to final archiving or technology transfer. Technology transfer studies explain the importance of innovation ecosystems and industry linkages, but many remain conceptual or policy-oriented and do not provide an integrated project-level software tool. In addition, research information management systems often focus on faculty publications and institutional productivity rather than student thesis and capstone projects. These limitations show that the problem is not simply the absence of digital tools, but the absence of a unified workflow that connects project inventory, milestone monitoring, evaluation, archiving, reporting, and partner engagement. The current study is justified by these gaps because it proposes a system that treats thesis and capstone projects as a complete lifecycle rather than as isolated records. The proposed Thesis and Capstone Project Inventory, Progress Monitoring, and Technology Transfer Management System intends to integrate repository management with progress monitoring, adviser and panel evaluation, dashboard reporting, and technology transfer tracking. By doing so, it addresses the storage limitations identified in repository systems, the follow-up weaknesses identified in monitoring studies, and the lack of operational tools identified in technology transfer literature. The system does not claim to guarantee external adoption of student projects, since industry acceptance depends on factors beyond the software itself. However, it can provide the institutional mechanism needed to make projects visible, organized, traceable, and easier to connect with potential partners. The reviewed literature presents a clear progression in the development of academic research management systems, beginning with digital repository and inventory platforms, expanding into monitoring and evaluation systems, and eventually progressing toward innovation and technology transfer initiatives. Studies on repository and inventory systems demonstrate that digital archiving greatly improves document accessibility, long-term preservation, and institutional record management. Over time, these systems have evolved from simple storage platforms into more advanced solutions that incorporate search functions, citation support, plagiarism detection, analytics, and secure data handling. Despite these developments, most repository systems still focus primarily on storing completed outputs rather than supporting research projects throughout their entire academic and administrative lifecycle. As a result, an important gap remains in managing research activities while projects are still in progress, not only after they have been completed. Furthermore , studies on monitoring and evaluation systems emphasize that academic progress and project completion improve when supervision, analytics, performance indicators, and project management practices are systematically organized. Existing literature shows that learning analytics can help identify students at risk of delays, productivity monitoring systems can support institutional reporting, and structured project management approaches can strengthen coordination in capstone courses. Although these systems contribute significantly to improving academic oversight, many were designed for specific purposes such as supervision management, course monitoring, institutional productivity analysis, or instructional support. Because of this, they often fail to provide a unified environment capable of handling the full thesis and capstone workflow from proposal development to final completion. In addition, even effective monitoring becomes limited when completed projects remain archived without opportunities for practical application or external collaboration. This limitation naturally leads to the growing importance of innovation and technology transfer systems, which aim to connect academic research with industry, government agencies, and community stakeholders. The reviewed studies emphasize the value of collaboration, commercialization, innovation ecosystems, and institutional partnerships in increasing the real-world impact of student research. However, many of these initiatives remain conceptual, policy-oriented, or focused mainly on institutional-level innovation management rather than providing practical systems that integrate repository management, progress monitoring, and technology transfer into a single platform. Consequently, although repository systems preserve knowledge, monitoring systems guide project completion, and technology transfer initiatives promote practical utilization, these functions are still commonly implemented as separate processes. The present study addresses this gap by proposing an integrated web-based platform that combines repository management, progress monitoring, evaluation support, and technology transfer into a unified system specifically designed for thesis and capstone management in higher education institutions, particularly within the USTP Jasaan Campus. To achieve the objectives of the study, the researchers will need to gather relevant data, including but not limited to: Current processes and practices in managing thesis and capstone projects Challenges experienced by students, advisers, and administrators in monitoring research progress and managing outputs Existing methods of storing, retrieving, and organizing research documents and records System requirements and user expectations for features such as inventory management, progress tracking, reporting, and technology transfer The data will be collected through appropriate data-gathering methods such as surveys , interviews , direct observation , and other necessary research techniques relevant to the study. All data gathered will be used strictly for academic purposes only. Rest assured that all information obtained during the conduct of the study will be treated with the utmost confidentiality. Participation will be voluntary, and no sensitive information will be disclosed without proper consent. In this regard, we respectfully request your approval to conduct the study in your organization. Your cooperation will greatly contribute to the successful completion of this research.
